# AI Tells in Website Copy

Patterns that make copy read as machine-written. Read this before the Final anti-AI pass,
then read the draft as a visitor would.

Adapted for marketing and website copy from [tropes.fyi](https://tropes.fyi) by
[ossama.is](https://ossama.is) and from [humanizer](https://github.com/blader/humanizer)
(MIT), which builds on Wikipedia's
["Signs of AI writing"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing).

**Contents:** Why these patterns appear · How strong is a tell · Staging instead of
stating · Rhythm by rule · Word choice · Tone · Formatting · Leftovers · Context-dependent ·
When not to act · Long-form only · Mechanical checks

## Why these patterns appear

A model picks the choice that fits the widest range of readers and products. A human
copywriter picks for one audience and one product, so their choices are uneven and
specific. Every pattern below is a default choice in disguise: a sentence that signals
importance instead of adding a fact, a shape applied by rule, an ordinary claim inflated.

So the test for any line is: **does it tell the visitor something they didn't already
have?** A sentence that restates the heading, the previous line or the obvious fails it,
however polished it sounds.

## How strong is a tell

Not every sighting needs an edit. A tell counts in proportion to how rarely a careful
writer would use it on purpose.

- **Strong: fix on one sighting.** Everything under "Staging instead of stating" and
  "Leftovers" below.
- **Weak alone: fix when stacked.** Everything else. One magic adverb or one triplet is
  normal writing. Several in one section, or the same one repeated down the page, is the
  tell.

Word habits change with every model release; structural habits persist. Prioritise
structure over vocabulary.

## Staging instead of stating

The strongest tells. The sentence stages a moment instead of making a claim.

**Negative parallelism.** "It's not X, it's Y" and its variants: "not just X, but Y",
"X rather than Y", and the same contrast split across two sentences ("This isn't another
CRM. It's a pipeline you'll actually update."). The most recognised AI tell. It frames
every claim as a correction of something nobody believed.
- ❌ "It's not a scheduler. It's your team's second brain."
- ✅ "Books meetings across time zones without the back-and-forth."

Keep a contrast only when the reader really does hold the belief being corrected, or when
both halves carry information.

**Clipped negative tail.** A short "no X" hung off the end of a sentence for weight:
"Pick a template and publish, no guesswork." Fold it into the sentence or cut it. The
exception is a tail that answers a real objection: "Start free, no credit card required"
tells the visitor something they wanted to know.

**Not X. Not Y. Just Z.** Negating two things before revealing the point.
- ❌ "Not another dashboard. Not another integration. Just answers."
- ✅ "Ask a question in plain English and get the number back."

**One-line closers.** A short line after a section that restates or names what the
section just showed: "That's the difference.", "Simple as that.", "Read that again.",
"The message is clear." Worse when the same closer ends several sections. Cut it, unless
it adds a fact the section didn't.

**Dramatic fragments and typographic shouting.** A row of fragments standing in for a
claim ("No setup. No learning curve. No excuses."), one word in ALL CAPS, or words split
by periods ("Every. Single. Day."). Merge the fragments into one sentence with a specific
claim. (A short fragment in a hero or tagline is fine; see Context-dependent.)

**Sayings that sound deep.** An ordinary point dressed as a hidden truth or aphorism:
"Great teams don't manage work. They move it.", "Trust is the currency of growth",
"Speed is the language of modern teams", "At its core, it's about people". If you can't
say what it claims, cut it; if you can, write that claim instead.

**Staged run-ups.** Announcing the point instead of making it: "Here's the thing.",
"But here's the catch.", "Here's what most teams miss.", "Let's be honest.", "Real talk:",
a standalone "Honestly?" before a routine claim. Delete the run-up and start at the point.

**False vulnerability.** "Okay, we'll be honest…", "And yes, we're obsessed with speed."
Performed candour. Real candour is specific, like saying what the product doesn't do yet.

**Arguing with no one.** Answering an objection nobody raised or rejecting an option
nobody proposed: "To be clear…", "Don't get me wrong…", "You might think you need another
tool. You don't.", "We're not saying spreadsheets are bad." Also "Despite its
challenges…", raising a problem only to dismiss it. Either answer a real objection
properly (pricing, contracts, migration) or leave it out.

## Rhythm by rule

**Stacked triplets.** One list of three is fine. Three tricolons in a row, triplets padded
with synonyms ("fast, quick and speedy"), or three parallel examples followed by a lesson
is a pattern. Use as many items as you actually have.

**Anaphora.** The same opening repeated in quick succession: "Built for founders. Built
for teams. Built for scale." Also several sentences in a row opening with "We" or "Our".

**Rhetorical question answered immediately.** "The result? Faster launches." "The best
part? It's free." The colon version is the same move: "The best part: it learns." State the
thing.

**Parallel bullet openers.** Every bullet starting with the same verb form: "Ensures… /
Provides… / Enables… / Delivers…". Start each with what the visitor gets, and let the
openings differ.

**Too many punchy moments.** A page gets one or two lines built to land. When every heading,
closer and caption tries to be memorable, none of them is, and the page reads as performed.

**Perfect balance.** Every section the same length, every feature card exactly two
sentences, every list three items. Real pages are uneven because some points need more
room than others.

**False ranges.** "From X to Y" where X and Y are not ends of a real scale.
- ❌ "From ideation to innovation to transformation."
- ✅ "From a single landing page to a 200-page docs site." (a real size range)

**Fake-depth -ing tails.** A trailing clause that attaches meaning without adding any:
"...empowering teams, fostering collaboration and driving outcomes." Watch for ensuring,
highlighting, reflecting, showcasing, cultivating, fostering. Cut the tail.

**Comma-clipped tails.** A short afterthought hung off a comma to close the sentence
("Connect your repo, and ship."). Land the point in the main clause.

**Stacked qualifiers.** "Could potentially help", "may arguably", "in many cases it can".
One honest hedge is fine; a chain of them makes every claim sound unsure. Keep the hedges
that are true and the ones legal or safety copy needs.

## Word choice

**The product as the actor.** Two related habits. Human verbs on software: "The dashboard
understands what your team needs", "Our AI knows when to follow up". Software shows,
sorts, flags and sends; it doesn't understand, know, decide, want or care. And abstract
subjects where the customer should be: "The platform empowers teams to ship" → "Your team
ships…". Make the visitor the subject when they're the one who benefits.
- ❌ "The platform intelligently understands your workflow."
- ✅ "You set the rules once; invoices over €5,000 go to finance automatically."

**Copula avoidance.** "Serves as", "stands as", "functions as", "represents", "marks".
Write "is". Likewise "boasts", "features", "offers" where "has" will do: "Every plan has
SSO", not "Every plan boasts SSO".

**Significance puffery.** "Marks a pivotal moment", "stands as a testament to",
"deeply rooted in", "unveil the secrets", "plays a key role in". Mundane facts dressed up
as history.

**Magic adverbs.** "Quietly", "deeply", "fundamentally", "remarkably", "truly",
"seamlessly", "effortlessly", "actually". Delete them and see if anything was lost.

**Inflated nouns.** Landscape, tapestry, ecosystem, paradigm, synergy, realm, journey,
game-changer, interplay, intricacies. Name the actual thing.

**Stock AI vocabulary.** Delve, leverage (verb), utilize, harness, robust, streamline,
elevate, unlock, unleash, empower, supercharge, enhance, bolster, garner, showcase,
underscore, highlight (verb), crucial, pivotal, key (adjective), valuable, meticulous,
vibrant, cutting-edge, next-level, world-class, powerful, ultimate, intelligent,
AI-powered (when it's the claim rather than a fact about how it works). These are tells
wherever they appear, and stronger in groups.

**Stock SaaS phrases.** "Whether you're a solo founder or a Fortune 500", "Say goodbye
to…", "X, reimagined", "Take your X to the next level", "in just a few clicks" (give the
real number of steps, or cut it).

**Promotional clichés.** Groundbreaking, seamless, bespoke, tailored, nestled, in the
heart of, renowned, stunning, breathtaking, a diverse array of, all-in-one solution,
unprecedented, "commitment to excellence", "passion for quality". Local-business sites
attract these most. Marketing copy may sell; it still has to say something concrete while
doing it.
- ❌ "A seamless, all-in-one solution that unlocks unprecedented productivity."
- ✅ "Invoices, payroll and expenses in one place, reconciled against your bank every night."
- ❌ "Nestled in the heart of the old town, our renowned studio is committed to excellence."
- ✅ "Our studio is on Mill Street, two doors from the market. We've been framing prints since 2009." (with the user's facts)

**Synonym cycling.** One referent, one word. If it's the "dashboard" in the hero, it is
not the "portal" in features and the "command center" in the CTA. Visitors read a new
word as a new thing.

**"Where it actually lives."** "Where the magic happens", "where your data lives".
Say what it is or where it is.

**Wordy filler.**
- "in order to" → "to"
- "at this point in time" → "now"
- "the ability to" → "can"
- "when it comes to", "in the realm of", "it's worth noting", "importantly",
  "interestingly", "notably", "additionally", "not only", "designed to enhance",
  "amongst" → delete or rewrite plainly

## Tone

**Grandiose stakes.** A scheduling tool does not "redefine how the world works". Scale
the claim to the product.

**Send-off endings.** Closing an About page or section on a forecast instead of a fact:
"The future looks bright.", "We're just getting started.", "Exciting times ahead."
End on the last concrete thing: what the visitor can do next, or a real plan the user gave
you.

**Vague attribution.** "Experts agree", "industry leaders trust", "studies show". Name the
source, quote the customer, or cut the claim. Never invent one. An "As seen in" logo row
is fine when the user supplied it; padding it out is not.

**Vague association.** "Associated with", "in partnership with", "connected to" where the
real relationship is known. Say whether it's a customer, an integration or a reseller. If
the user didn't say, ask or leave it vague; don't pick one.

**Invented concept labels.** "The productivity paradox", "the handoff trap", "tool creep"
used as if they were established terms. Describe the problem instead of naming it.

**Performed cleverness.** Wordplay standing in for a claim, usually in headlines: "Your
inbox, minus the noise", "Meetings, but make them useful". It shows up after a first
de-slop pass, once the obvious clichés are gone. Ask what it promises, and say that.

**Never admitting a limit.** Copy that's breezy and sure about everything, with no edge
cases, no "not for you if…", no setup time. Real products have limits, and saying one
(that the user confirmed) is what makes the rest believable.

**Forced figurative language.** Metaphors reached for because they sound clever: "a Swiss
Army knife for your pipeline", "think of it as air traffic control for your inbox". Use a
comparison only when the plain version is genuinely harder to grasp.

**"Imagine a world where…"** Show the product doing the job instead.

**Appeal to familiarity.** "As every marketer knows", "famously", "a classic". Unproven
consensus.

**Rapid-fire analogies.** "Stripe did it for payments. Figma did it for design. We're doing
it for X." Borrowed authority.

## Formatting

**Em dashes.** Two or three per page is human. One per paragraph is not. Use a period,
comma, colon or parentheses. If the user's own copy uses dashes freely, match their rate
instead.

**Title case.** Headings, buttons and nav use sentence case: "Start free trial", not
"Start Free Trial". Proper nouns and product names keep their capitals.

**Wh-headers everywhere.** "What we do", "Why teams switch", "How it works". One is fine.
When every section heading starts with a Wh-word, rewrite most as statements of the benefit.

**Heading echoed by the first line.** A heading followed by a sentence that restates it
before the real content starts.
- ❌ "## Built for speed" → "Speed matters." → "Pages load in under a second."
- ✅ "## Built for speed" → "Pages load in under a second, even on mobile data."

The same goes for an eyebrow (the small label above an H1) that repeats the category the
headline already names: "INVOICING SOFTWARE" above "Invoicing for freelancers". Use the
eyebrow for something the headline doesn't say, or drop it.

**Exclamation points.** One per page, at most, and never on a heading or button.

**Scare quotes.** Quotation marks around ordinary words for emphasis or irony: Our "simple"
setup, no more "busywork". Remove them; quote only what someone actually said.

**Compulsive counting.** "Three reasons teams switch", "5 features you'll love". Leave the
count out unless the number itself is the point.

**Decorative emoji and arrows.** An emoji on every heading or bullet (🚀 Launch faster,
💡 Smart insights), or "Upload → Edit → Publish" in running copy. Write it as a sentence,
or use a real step list. (Icons from the design system and arrow icons on buttons and
links are fine.)

## Leftovers

Strong tells. Remove them outright; nothing here needs rewriting.

**Chatbot residue in the copy.** Text meant for the user that ended up in the page: "Here's
a hero section that highlights…", "Feel free to adjust the tone", "I hope this works for
your brand", "[Insert compelling tagline]". The only bracketed or note-like text allowed
in shipped copy is a `TODO:` placeholder.

**Guess-filling.** Covering a missing fact with a plausible guess: "Founded over a decade
ago", "likely trusted by thousands", "it is believed that". That is an invented fact. Use
a `TODO:` placeholder.

**Copy about the page.** Describing the page instead of the product: "Below you'll find
our pricing", "This page walks you through our features", "Scroll down to learn more".
The visitor can see the page. Say the thing.

## Context-dependent

These are legitimate in website copy. Flag them only in the stated case.

**Short fragments.** Fine in a hero, a tagline or a CTA line. A tell when whole
paragraphs are built from them ("Flaky tests. Slow builds. Endless reviews.") or every
section opens the same way.

**"We" voice.** Correct for a company site. Wrong for a personal portfolio, a founder's
letter or anything the user wrote in "I". Match the voice the user established.

**Bold-first bullets.** Standard in feature grids and comparison lists. A tell in running
prose, or when every list on the page uses it and the bold labels just repeat what the
sentence after them says.

**Pain-point questions.** "Still chasing approvals over email?" works once, as a hook.
Two or more per page reads as a template.

**Curly quotes.** Correct typography on a rendered site. Not a tell here.

**Fragments without a subject.** "No configuration needed." is normal in feature cards and
microcopy. Not a tell here.

## When not to act

Any single pattern can be a deliberate choice, and people judging by feel do little better
than chance. Several tells together are the safeguard. Leave a watched phrase alone when:

- It's inside a customer testimonial, review or quote. Those are the customer's words and
  are never edited for style.
- It's a proper name: a product called "Seamless", a plan called "Pro Unlimited".
- The user wrote it and didn't ask for edits (see "When to write, when to preserve" in
  `SKILL.md`).

And keep the details that make copy sound like a person, even when they look untidy: a
specific odd fact ("we started in a converted bike shop"), a real limitation stated
plainly, a genuine aside in parentheses, a founder's opinion.

## Long-form only

These rarely show up in a hero or a feature card, but do on About pages, founder notes and
long landing pages:

- Preamble: announcing what's next ("Here's what makes us different") instead of saying it.
- Signposted or tied-back conclusions: "In short…", "To sum up…", "So, to answer the question…"
- Never-ending closer: stacking clause after clause instead of one final line.
- Premise stacking: giving the evidence for a point three times before making it.
- Belabouring: defending a point nobody would dispute.
- One-point dilution: the same claim restated section after section with new metaphors.
- Fractal summaries: every section opening with a preview and closing with a recap.
- Enumerated prose: "The first reason… The second reason…" as a list disguised as paragraphs.
- Reasoning leak: the copy narrating its own intent ("We want to be clear about this").
- Self-echo: the same distinctive word paying itself off across the page.

## Mechanical checks

Reading as a visitor catches most tells; these searches catch what the reading misses. Run
them over the draft after the rewrite:

- **Headlines and subheads:** search for `not `, `isn't`, `no `, `without`, `?`, `:` and `—`.
  Each hit is a likely contrast, rhetorical question, colon reveal or dash. Keep only the
  ones that carry information.
- **Long sentences:** any sentence over 20 words with more than two commas is probably a
  stacked list or a fake-depth tail. Split it or cut the tail.
- **Openings:** list the first word of every heading, bullet and section. Repeats show
  anaphora, parallel bullet openers and Wh-headers at a glance.
- **Section lengths:** compare them. If they're all the same, some point is padded or cut
  short.
- **Em dashes and exclamation points:** count them against the limits above.
