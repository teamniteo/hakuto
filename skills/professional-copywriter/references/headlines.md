# Headline Formulas and Rewrites

Starting shapes for drafting headlines, and the ways each one goes wrong. Read this when
drafting candidates (step 2 of the headline process in `SKILL.md`). A formula gets you a
first draft; the headline tests in `SKILL.md` decide whether it ships.

Every example product below is made up. Numbers in examples stand for the user's numbers:
a real headline uses figures the user gave, or carries a `TODO:`.

**Contents:** Pick by awareness · Formulas · Hero anatomy · Section headings ·
Common failures · Scoring · Sources

## Pick by awareness

Eugene Schwartz's awareness levels decide how directly a headline can talk about the
product. The less the visitor knows, the more the headline has to open with something they
already recognise: their problem or the outcome they want.

| The visitor... | Usually arrives on | Lead with | Formulas that fit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Feels the problem, doesn't know solutions exist | Non-brand ads, problem-led landing pages, how-to content | The pain or outcome, in their words | 2, 3, 5, 9, 12, 13 |
| Knows solutions exist, not this one | Homepage, category search, use-case pages | Category and what's different | 1, 2, 4, 7, 10 |
| Knows this product, is comparing | Pricing, "X vs Y", feature pages, return visits | The differentiator, the offer, risk removal | 6, 8, 11, 14, 15 |

Most homepage visitors arrive through the brand name or a referral, so they're usually in
the middle row. When unsure, write for the middle row.

## Formulas

### 1. Category for audience, or category that does X
`The {category} for {audience}` · `{Category} that {differentiator}` · `{Category} for {audience who has a situation}`

- **Fits:** homepage heroes; the default for any product that isn't yet famous, because it
  tells the visitor what shelf to put it on (April Dunford's positioning work).
- **Examples:** "The CRM for solo founders". "Payroll for EU startups with contractors in
  ten countries". "Invoicing that chases late payers for you".
- **Fails when** the differentiator is generic ("modern", "all-in-one", "AI-powered") or the
  category is invented and nobody searches for it.

### 2. Outcome by mechanism
`{Outcome the visitor wants} by {how the product does it}`

- **Fits:** problem- and solution-aware visitors; heroes and feature headings. The
  mechanism is what makes the outcome believable.
- **Example:** "Close the books in two days by matching every transaction as it lands".
- **Fails when** the outcome is abstract ("Unlock growth by…") or the mechanism is a
  buzzword ("…with the power of AI").

### 3. Outcome without the pain
`{Outcome} without {the specific pain they expect}`

- **Fits:** problem-aware visitors who already expect a cost: setup, migration, an engineer,
  a contract.
- **Examples:** "Ship on Fridays without breaking production". "Run payroll in 30 countries
  without opening a local entity".
- **Fails when** nobody expected that pain. Then it's arguing with no one, a strong AI tell
  (see `ai-tells.md`). The "without" half must be something visitors really say.

### 4. Hard task in a timeframe
`{Hard task} in {real timeframe}`

- **Fits:** when speed is the actual difference and the user can stand behind the number.
- **Examples:** "Launch a help centre in an afternoon". iPod's "1,000 songs in your pocket"
  is the same shape with capacity instead of time.
- **Fails when** the timeframe isn't the user's (it's an invented fact) or only holds for
  the simplest case.

### 5. How to
`How to {achieve outcome} {without / even if} {constraint}`

- **Fits:** lead magnets, guides, problem-led landing pages.
- **Example:** "How to onboard 50 contractors without a spreadsheet".
- **Fails when** used as a product hero. It reads like a blog post title.

### 6. Proof as headline
`{Real number} {audience} {outcome}` · or a customer quote as the headline

- **Fits:** product-aware visitors, pricing and case-study pages, when the number or quote is
  strong on its own.
- **Examples:** "4,180 bookkeepers close month-end here". "'We stopped losing invoices in
  the first week.' Maria Lopez, office manager".
- **Fails when** the number is small or a vanity metric, the quote is generic praise, or the
  proof isn't the user's. Never write this formula with an invented number; use a
  `TODO:` and pick another formula for the draft.

### 7. Audience call-out
`For {specific role} who {specific situation}`

- **Fits:** segmented landing pages, use-case pages, products for a narrow group.
- **Example:** "For agencies running 30 client sites from one login".
- **Fails when** the audience is broad ("For teams of all sizes"). Then it qualifies no one.

### 8. Now you can
`Now you can {thing that used to be impossible or painful}`

- **Fits:** launches, feature and changelog pages: real news.
- **Example:** "Now you can approve expenses from the Slack thread they came up in".
- **Fails when** the "news" is old to the visitor. Even when you don't publish it, this is
  the "Now you can…" test in `SKILL.md`: a good headline reads as true after it.

### 9. Question the visitor answers yes to
`{Still / Tired of} {specific situation they're in}?`

- **Fits:** problem-aware visitors; better on section headings and ads than on heroes.
- **Example:** "Still chasing approvals over email?"
- **Fails when** the visitor can answer "no" and leave, or there's more than one per page.
  Evidence that questions win or lose is mixed; use one when the situation is sharp.

### 10. Familiar thing, with the difference
`{Familiar product} for {new job}` · `Like {familiar thing}, but {specific difference}`

- **Fits:** a new kind of product that's hard to explain cold. Anchoring to something known
  is the fastest way to get the idea across.
- **Examples:** "A shared inbox for invoices". "Like Google Docs, but every edit is
  versioned for auditors".
- **Fails when** it turns into wordplay ("Like a calendar. Unlike any calendar.") with no
  difference stated. That's performed cleverness.

### 11. Objection first
`{Thing they fear is hard} {in a way that removes it}`

- **Fits:** product-aware visitors with one dominant blocker: price, migration, security,
  team buy-in. Ask the user what almost stopped customers from buying.
- **Examples:** "Move from Jira in an afternoon. We migrate it for you". "SSO and audit logs
  on every plan".
- **Fails when** it raises a worry the visitor didn't have, or takes on several at once.

### 12. Before and after
`From {current state} to {better state}`

- **Fits:** problem-aware visitors; heroes and section headings, best with a visual showing
  the change.
- **Example:** "From 40 open tabs to one list of what's overdue".
- **Fails when** the "before" is a strawman nobody recognises, or the "after" is abstract
  ("…to clarity").

### 13. Stop doing the painful thing
`Stop {specific recurring chore}`

- **Fits:** a sharp, recurring frustration the visitor names themselves.
- **Example:** "Stop rebuilding the same report every Monday".
- **Fails when** there's no clear replacement in the subhead, or it's used more than once.
  "Stop X. Start Y." pairs drift into the "not X, it's Y" tell; one half is usually enough.

### 14. Stated difference against the alternative
`{Category} {that does the thing the alternatives refuse to}`

- **Fits:** crowded categories where the user has a real stance (pricing model, a feature
  they leave out on purpose).
- **Example:** "Project management with no per-seat pricing".
- **Fails when** the stance is borrowed or the "alternative" is a strawman. Don't attack the
  visitor's current tool (see "Don't tell visitors they chose wrong" in `SKILL.md`).

### 15. Offer and risk removal
`{What they get} {terms that take away the risk}`

- **Fits:** pricing pages, closing CTA sections, most-aware visitors.
- **Example:** "Every feature, free for 30 days. Export your data whenever you like".
- **Fails when** the terms aren't the user's real ones.

### Shapes to avoid for an unknown brand

- **Product name plus specs** ("Acme. Thinner. Lighter."): works for brands visitors
  already know; for anyone else it hides the category.
- **Quality claims** ("A better way to build products"): "better" can't be proven and every
  competitor can say it.
- **"Who else wants…"** and other mail-order shapes: read as dated in B2B.

## Hero anatomy

The hero blocks split the work. Together they answer three questions above the fold: what
is it, who is it for, why should I care (Joel Klettke's first three visitor questions).

| Block | Length | Job | Fails when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Eyebrow (optional) | 2-5 words | Category or audience, when the H1 doesn't carry it | It says "New" or "Welcome", or repeats the H1 |
| H1 | about 4-12 words | One idea: the main benefit or difference | It holds two ideas, or only makes sense after the subhead |
| Subhead | 1-2 sentences | How it works and why the H1 is believable; who it's for if the H1 didn't say | It restates the H1 or lists three adjectives |
| CTA + line under it | Button + one line | The next step, and the risk removed | See CTAs in `SKILL.md` |

❌ **Weak:**
> Eyebrow: WELCOME TO LEDGERLY
> H1: Smarter finance for modern teams
> Subhead: Ledgerly is the all-in-one platform that empowers your team to work smarter, faster and better.

The H1 has no category and fails the picture test, and the subhead repeats it with three
adjectives. A visitor still doesn't know what Ledgerly does.

✅ **Strong:**
> Eyebrow: Bookkeeping for freelancers
> H1: Your books are done before your accountant asks
> Subhead: Ledgerly matches every bank transaction to an invoice or receipt as it lands, so month-end is an hour of checking what it matched.

The eyebrow places it, the H1 makes one promise a visitor can picture, and the subhead
says how that's possible.

## Section headings

Most visitors read the headings and skim the rest. Read the H2s alone, top to bottom: they
should tell the pitch. Labels tell the visitor where they are, not why to keep reading.

| Label | Claim |
|---|---|
| Features | Everything you need to bill a client, in one screen |
| Integrations | Works with the bank and the accounting tool you already use |
| Security | SOC 2 Type II, SSO and audit logs on every plan *(only if the user confirmed each)* |
| How it works | Set up in three steps, no engineer needed |
| Why us | The only scheduler that books across time zones without a call |

Keep labels where the visitor is looking something up rather than being persuaded: FAQ,
Pricing, Docs, Contact, navigation. A claim heading on a FAQ just slows the search down.

Front-load the noun that matters. Phone widths cut a heading at about 25-30 characters per
line; the first line may be all a visitor reads.

## Common failures

Headlines are where AI tells and generic claims concentrate. Most rejected candidates fail
on one of these:

1. **Abstraction.** "Unlock your potential", "Work smarter", "The future of invoicing". Can't
   be pictured or proved.
2. **Wordplay.** Puns and "X, but make it Y" shapes. They fail for non-native readers and
   out of context (search results, link previews).
3. **Stock vocabulary.** Seamless, robust, all-in-one, revolutionise, supercharge, empower,
   next-generation, AI-powered (when AI is how, not what you get).
4. **Feature without outcome.** "Real-time sync and role-based permissions". Put the outcome
   in the H1, the feature in the subhead. (Technical buyers are the exception; see
   `SKILL.md`.)
5. **Unprovable superlatives.** "The best", "#1", "world-class", "the fastest". Replace with
   the user's specific proof, or a `TODO:` naming what would back it.
6. **About the company.** "We are a leading provider of…", "Welcome to Acme". In MECLABS's
   test, swapping a company-centred headline for one stating the visitor's value doubled
   conversions.
7. **Two ideas in one.** Commas stacking benefits: "Faster invoicing, happier clients and
   fewer late payments".
8. **Too short for an unknown brand.** Three-word minimalism with no category.
9. **Hidden category.** The most common finding in B2B message tests: visitors can't place
   the product.
10. **Overpromising.** A headline the page then can't back loses the visitor's trust for the
    rest of the page.
11. **Subhead repeats the H1.** A wasted line that could have said how.

## Scoring

When several candidates pass the tests in `SKILL.md`, rank them on three of the
direct-response "4 U's" (urgency rarely applies to SaaS heroes, and invented urgency is an
invented fact):

- **Useful:** how much the visitor wants the outcome
- **Unique:** how few competitors could publish it unchanged
- **Ultra-specific:** how concretely it can be pictured or checked

The highest total wins, and ties go to the plainer line.

## Sources

Consensus from David Ogilvy and John Caples (self-interest beats curiosity; one idea),
Eugene Schwartz (awareness levels), April Dunford (category first), Nielsen Norman Group
(front-loaded, plain, stand-alone headings; puns hurt), Julian Shapiro (hero and subhead
roles), Joanna Wiebe and Joel Klettke (customer language, visitor questions), Harry Dry
(picture it, prove it, only us) and MECLABS (clarity over company-centred copy). Popular
figures like "5x as many read the headline" and "odd numbers convert better" are folklore;
don't repeat them as facts.
